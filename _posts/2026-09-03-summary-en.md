This finding, published in Nature on September 2, 2026, challenges the traditional view that ribosomes only translate external mRNA. This discovery fundamentally challenges our understanding of translation and the central dogma of molecular biology, suggesting that ribosomes may have self-coding capabilities. It could have profound implications for synthetic biology, enabling new approaches to engineer self-replicating genetic systems, and for understanding the early evolution of life. The study engineered a chimeric messenger-ribosomal RNA (mrRNA) by appending a protein-coding sequence to 16S ribosomal RNA, and showed that bacterial ribosomes can translate this mrRNA to produce functional proteins. The research was published in Nature on September 2, 2026, with the DOI 10.1038/s41586-026-10962-1.

rss · Nature - Latest Research · Sep 2, 00:00

**Background**: Ribosomes are complex molecular machines that synthesize proteins by translating messenger RNA (mRNA) sequences into amino acid chains. Traditionally, ribosomes are thought to translate only external mRNA, not their own RNA. This discovery suggests that ribosomes can also translate RNA that is part of their own structure, which could have implications for the evolution of the genetic code and the origin of life.

## [Inflammation-induced chimeric mRNAs encode functional proteins in immunity](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-10982-x) ⭐️ 8.0/10

A new study published in Nature reveals that inflammation triggers interchromosomal DNA interactions, bringing parent genes into proximity and facilitating the formation of chimeric mRNAs that encode functional proteins relevant to mammalian immunity. This discovery challenges the traditional view that chimeric RNAs are primarily associated with cancer or chromosomal rearrangements, suggesting a regulated mechanism in normal physiology. It could reshape our understanding of gene regulation and immune responses, potentially opening new avenues for therapeutic interventions. The study was published online on September 2, 2026, with DOI 10.1038/s41586-026-10982-x. The chimeric mRNAs are formed from genes on different chromosomes, and the resulting proteins are physiologically relevant, indicating a novel layer of immune regulation.

rss · Nature - Latest Research · Sep 2, 00:00

**Background**: Chimeric RNAs, also known as fusion transcripts, are composed of exons from two or more different genes and can encode novel proteins. They are typically formed through chromosomal rearrangements or trans-splicing, and have been extensively studied in cancer. Interchromosomal interactions, where different chromosomes come into close proximity in the nucleus, are known to influence gene expression, but their role in generating functional chimeric mRNAs in normal physiology was previously unclear.
