## [Vacuum fluctuations enhance superconductivity in NbSe2](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-11037-x) ⭐️ 9.0/10

A Nature paper published online on August 19, 2026 reports experimental evidence that vacuum fluctuations can enhance superconductivity in the layered material NbSe2. This is the first demonstration that vacuum conditions alone can boost superconducting properties. This discovery could open new avenues for controlling material properties noninvasively, potentially leading to improved superconducting materials for practical applications. It challenges existing theoretical frameworks and may inspire further research into cavity quantum electrodynamics and condensed matter physics. The study focuses on NbSe2, a layered superconductor, and provides experimental evidence that vacuum fluctuations enhance its superconductivity. A separate arXiv paper (2608.14784) presents a no-go theorem for vacuum-enhanced superconductivity within minimal cavity electrodynamics, suggesting that additional physics is needed to explain such enhancement.

**Background**: Superconductivity is a quantum phenomenon where certain materials conduct electricity with zero resistance at low temperatures. Vacuum fluctuations are transient changes in the energy of empty space due to quantum mechanics. The idea that vacuum fluctuations could influence material properties is relatively new, and this study provides experimental support for that concept in the context of superconductivity.

## [Quantum Simulator Directly Observes Conformal Field Theory Spectra](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-10904-x) ⭐️ 9.0/10

Researchers directly observed the universal excitation spectra predicted by conformal field theory (CFT) using an array of optically trapped neutral atoms at a quantum phase transition. The experiment, published in Nature on August 19, 2026, measured finite-size many-body excitation spectra at one-dimensional quantum phase transitions governed by Ising and tricritical Ising CFTs. This is a major breakthrough in quantum simulation and many-body physics, as it provides the first direct experimental observation of CFT spectra, which are fundamental to understanding quantum critical phenomena. The results could impact both fundamental physics and the development of quantum computing, as they demonstrate the power of programmable quantum simulators to probe universal properties of matter. The experiment used a programmable neutral atom quantum simulator to measure the finite-size excitation spectrum at one-dimensional quantum phase transitions, which are described by two-dimensional (1+1D space-time) Ising and tricritical Ising CFTs. The observed energy ratios matched the universal predictions of these CFTs, confirming the emergent conformal symmetry.

rss · Nature - Latest Research · Aug 19, 00:00

**Background**: Conformal field theory (CFT) is a quantum field theory that is invariant under conformal transformations, which include scale transformations. CFTs play a crucial role in describing critical phenomena and quantum phase transitions, where systems exhibit scale invariance. Quantum phase transitions occur at zero temperature and are driven by quantum fluctuations, unlike classical phase transitions driven by thermal fluctuations. Neutral atom quantum simulators use arrays of atoms trapped by optical tweezers to emulate quantum many-body systems, allowing the study of phenomena that are difficult to probe in traditional experiments.

## [Single-cell mtDNA artefacts mislead phylogenetic trees](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-10777-0) ⭐️ 8.0/10

A Nature article published online on 19 August 2026 reports that artefacts in single-cell mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) analyses can produce incorrect phylogenetic trees, urging caution in interpreting such data. The article is accompanied by a reply, indicating an active scientific debate. This finding is critical for the field of single-cell genomics and phylogenetics, as it challenges the reliability of lineage tracing and clonal inference based on mtDNA variants. It could lead to revised methodologies and more rigorous validation in studies using single-cell mtDNA data, affecting cancer research, developmental biology, and evolutionary studies. The pre-print on bioRxiv (2024) identified that shared variants between cells are disproportionately detected in a single molecule per cell and are enriched 10–20-fold at the edges of mtDNA molecules, reminiscent of sequencing artefacts. Pruning low-support and likely artificial variants removes nearly all reported phylogenetic structure, suggesting that many inferred lineages may be artefacts.

rss · Nature - Latest Research · Aug 19, 00:00

**Background**: Single-cell mtDNA sequencing is used to track cell lineages and clonal relationships by detecting heteroplasmic variants. However, artefacts from library preparation, sequencing errors, or amplification biases can create false variants that distort phylogenetic inference. The bioRxiv preprint and related studies highlight the need for careful filtering and validation of mtDNA variants.
