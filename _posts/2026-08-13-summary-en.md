## [Lead carboxylates passivation enables meter-scale perovskite solar modules](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-10994-7) ⭐️ 9.0/10

Researchers demonstrated that chemically stable lead carboxylates can effectively passivate perovskite films with a naturally-formed formamidinium iodide-enriched surface, enabling the fabrication of meter-scale perovskite solar modules under ambient conditions. This breakthrough was published in Nature on August 12, 2026. This advance addresses a critical challenge in scaling perovskite solar cells from laboratory to commercial production, as large-area modules have previously suffered from non-uniform passivation and performance losses. Achieving meter-scale modules with effective passivation is a major step toward the commercial viability of perovskite solar technology, which could significantly impact the renewable energy industry. The passivation technique uses lead carboxylates on perovskite films engineered with a high-saturation vapor pressure solvent system (2-methoxyethanol, 1,3-dioxolane, and dimethyl sulfoxide) to create a formamidinium iodide-enriched surface. The study highlights that applying surface treatments to meter-scale modules under ambient conditions is challenging due to the heterogeneous distribution of passivators during large-area slot-die coating.

rss · Nature - Latest Research · Aug 12, 00:00

**Background**: Perovskite solar cells are a promising photovoltaic technology that uses a perovskite-structured compound, typically a hybrid organic-inorganic lead or tin halide, as the light-harvesting layer. These cells are susceptible to defects (trap states) that reduce efficiency, and passivation techniques are used to overcome these defects and improve charge-carrier lifetime and performance. Scaling up to large-area modules requires uniform crystallization and passivation, which has been a major hurdle.

## [Luminescent-reaction-enabled super-resolution imaging](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-10889-7) ⭐️ 9.0/10

A new chemistry-based super-resolution imaging framework, published in Nature on August 12, 2026, enables 3D, laser-free, highly sensitive, and ultralong-term live-cell imaging. It overcomes previous spatiotemporal resolution limits of reaction-based imaging methods. This breakthrough could transform live-cell imaging by allowing researchers to observe dynamic biological processes over extended periods without phototoxicity from lasers. It has broad potential applications in cell biology, neuroscience, and drug discovery. The framework uses a spatiotemporal isolation strategy to monitor reaction-based luminescence processes in image sequences, achieving super-resolution. According to a related preprint, the method (RIED) enables 3D super-resolution ECL imaging with ~97 nm lateral and ~235 nm axial resolution.

rss · Nature - Latest Research · Aug 12, 00:00

**Background**: Super-resolution microscopy techniques, such as STED and STORM, typically require high-intensity lasers, which can cause photobleaching and phototoxicity, limiting long-term live-cell imaging. Reaction-based imaging methods, like chemiluminescence or electrochemiluminescence (ECL), offer lower background and no excitation light, but have suffered from poor spatiotemporal resolution. This new framework addresses that limitation by chemically enabling super-resolution without lasers.

## [Pristine Monolayer Cuprate Superconductor Reveals Quantum Critical Phenomena](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-10857-1) ⭐️ 9.0/10

Researchers have successfully fabricated a pristine monolayer cuprate superconductor with a single CuO2 plane, and used it to identify two quantum critical phenomena emerging between the Mott insulating phase and the superconducting phase. This work was published in Nature on August 12, 2026. This breakthrough enables direct experimental study of quantum criticality in high-temperature superconductors, which could reshape our understanding of the mechanism behind high-temperature superconductivity. It provides a clean platform for testing theoretical models and may guide the design of new superconducting materials. The monolayer cuprate was fabricated with high quality and purity, allowing the observation of two distinct quantum critical points. The study focuses on the transition between the Mott insulating phase and the superconducting phase, which is a central issue in condensed matter physics.

rss · Nature - Latest Research · Aug 12, 00:00

**Background**: Cuprates are a class of materials that exhibit high-temperature superconductivity, but their mechanism remains elusive. A key feature is the Mott insulating phase, where strong electron correlations cause the material to be an insulator despite a partially filled band. Quantum critical points are points where quantum fluctuations drive a phase transition at absolute zero, and they are believed to play a role in the physics of cuprates. Fabricating a pristine monolayer is challenging due to surface and interface effects, making this achievement significant.

## [Physicists Detect Elusive Glueball, Confirming Decades-Old Prediction](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-026-02498-1) ⭐️ 9.0/10

A team of physicists in China, part of the BESIII collaboration, has reported strong evidence for the glueball, a particle composed entirely of gluons. The announcement, made on August 5, 2026, identifies the particle X(2370) as the first experimentally confirmed glueball. This breakthrough confirms a long-standing prediction of quantum chromodynamics (QCD), the theory of the strong force, and provides direct evidence for the self-interaction of gluons. It could deepen our understanding of the strong force and the nature of matter, potentially impacting particle physics and cosmology. The glueball candidate, X(2370), was detected at the Beijing Electron Positron Collider (BEPC) by the BESIII collaboration. The identification required decades of searching and analysis, and the result closes a fifty-year search for this particle.

rss · Nature - Latest Research · Aug 12, 00:00

**Background**: In particle physics, a glueball is a hypothetical composite particle consisting solely of gluons, without valence quarks. Gluons are the force carriers of the strong interaction, which binds quarks into protons, neutrons, and other hadrons. Because gluons carry color charge, they can interact with each other, leading to the possibility of bound states made entirely of gluons. The existence of glueballs is a key prediction of quantum chromodynamics (QCD), the theory describing the strong force.

## [Dataset Artefacts Partly Explain Declining Disruption](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-10787-y) ⭐️ 8.0/10

A new Nature paper (published online 12 August 2026) reports that dataset artefacts can partially drive the measured decline in scientific disruption, challenging previous conclusions. The paper is accompanied by a reply from the original authors, indicating an ongoing scientific debate. This finding is significant because it questions the widely reported claim that science is becoming less disruptive, which has implications for research policy and funding decisions. If the decline is partly an artefact, then the perceived slowdown in breakthrough science may be overstated, affecting how we evaluate scientific progress. The paper identifies specific dataset artefacts, such as missing references in certain records, that can skew disruption index calculations. The authors note that most records do contain references, but the artefacts still weaken the measured disruptiveness, suggesting the effect is real but partially driven by data quality issues.

rss · Nature - Latest Research · Aug 12, 00:00

**Background**: The disruption index (D-index) is a bibliometric measure that quantifies how much a paper disrupts prior work by displacing citations to its references. It was introduced by Funk and Owen-Smith (2017) for patents and later applied to scientific publications by Wu et al. (2019). A declining trend in D-index across many fields has been interpreted as a sign that science is becoming less innovative, but this new study suggests that artefacts in the underlying datasets may contribute to that trend.
